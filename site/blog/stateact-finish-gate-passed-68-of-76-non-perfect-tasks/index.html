StateAct's finish gate never saw the agent's account, and it still passed 68 of 76 non-perfect tasks

StateAct (arXiv:2607.22798, Salesforce AI Research, submitted 24 July 2026) ends each OSWorld 2.0 task with a finish gate that sees the task instruction and the machine, and nothing the agent said. Table 5 scores that gate against the benchmark grader. Of 79 non-perfect tasks, 76 reached the gate. It rejected 8 and passed 68. The paper calls this "an error rate of 68/76" and rounds it to 90%.

"StateAct: Program State, before Pixels, for Long-Horizon Computer-Use Agents" is by Yan Yang, Xiangru Jian, Ziyang Luo and seven co-authors. Its headline is that acting on files, application backends and the DOM through code, with a GUI subagent for the few steps that need a screen, lifts Claude Opus 4.8 on OSWorld 2.0 from 20.6% to 26.9% binary success and from 54.8% to 61.6% partial, at about $7.8 per task against about $72 for the reference computer-use setup. The $72 is cited from the benchmark's paper and was not re-measured. This note is about the verification layer, where the paper is unusually direct about its own ceiling.

Table 5 is a confusion matrix for a verifier, and most papers do not print one

The gate fires when the main agent calls finish. Section 4.2 describes its context: the verbatim task instruction plus bash, file reads and DOM queries, with editor writes blocked. The sentence that matters: "It never sees the main agent's message history, plan, or finish rationale, nor the expected values." It may reject up to three times.

Read as a classifier of "is this task done correctly," the gate has high precision when it objects (8 of 9) and low recall (8 of 76). It wrongly rejected 1 of 29 binary successes. Three non-perfect tasks never called finish. The ablation in Table 3a puts a value on it: without the gate, binary success is 23.1% against 26.9% with it, a difference of 3.8 points, which on 108 tasks is about 4 tasks. Table 5b agrees in order of magnitude: 6 of 28 retries ended in binary success. No run counts or intervals are reported for any configuration.

Blindness to the agent's story removes one bias and leaves the larger one

The gate was built to avoid agreeing with a persuasive narration, and the paper cites prior work on that bias. It then reports that independence was not enough: the 68 passes are "a common-mode failure: shared interpretation of the source." The gate re-reads the same spreadsheet or calendar store under the same reading of the instruction and reproduces the agent's wrong answer. By the paper's count "only 14 are misses a structural check could have caught," which leaves 54 that fail on value or reasoning. Its conclusion: "without ground-truth labels, a prompt-level verifier bounds structural, not value, correctness."

That lines up with the failure audit in Section 6.1, where the 79 non-perfect tasks split into 38 agent-reasoning failures, 14 verifier-weak or wrong-path cases, 22 that hit a modality limit or an ambiguous instruction, and 5 left undecomposed. Those labels are manual and no second-rater agreement is given.

Two other figures in the abstract need their definitions attached. The GUI subagent is used on "just 28 of 108 tasks and 1.1% of main-agent steps"; Section 4.1 adds that the share rises "to ~11% of total model turns once the subagent's interior turns are included." And the "code-only variant with no GUI subagent" at 45.9% partial is Table 4's bash-only configuration, defined as "no GUI, subagents, or finish gate," so three things were removed, not one. StateAct also takes more model turns than the reference, 155 against 103 per task. It is cheaper per task and longer.

What it means for anyone grading agent work without an answer key

Yesterday we covered a study in which a judge shown the agent's success claim caught half as many false successes as a blind one. StateAct is the complementary data point. Its gate is blind by construction and it still passes about nine in ten imperfect results, because the missing ingredient is the expected value. Hiding the agent's account from the checker is necessary. This table shows it is far from sufficient.

The paper names the alternative: "hard-coded per-task verifiers" win on this axis "at the cost of generality." That is the trade phoneshell made. Each of our 80 tasks ships with checks whose expected values are written before the run, and phoneshell/bench/verify.py states the requirement for the one check that reads the model's answer: "Ground truth has to be fixed and known in advance." The cost is real. We can grade 80 tasks and nothing outside them, and a gate like StateAct's works on a task it has never seen. What Table 5 measures is the price of that generality: a general gate is worth about 4 tasks in 108 here, and its PASS carries little information, since 68 of its 96 passes went to non-perfect work.

For a deployed agent the practical reading is narrow. Keep a structural finish check, because its rejections are right 8 times in 9 and cost little. Do not report its pass rate as a success rate.

What would prove this wrong

We claim that Table 5 of arXiv:2607.22798v1 shows a narration-blind finish gate passing 68 of 76 non-perfect tasks, and that the cause is the absence of expected values. Our prediction: any paper published by 30 June 2027 that reports a confusion matrix for a prompt-level completion verifier with no access to expected values, on OSWorld 2.0 or a benchmark of similar horizon, will show it rejecting fewer than 25% of the non-perfect tasks that reach it. A verifier of that kind rejecting half or more, with a false-rejection rate under 10% on correct tasks, would show the ceiling is not intrinsic.
