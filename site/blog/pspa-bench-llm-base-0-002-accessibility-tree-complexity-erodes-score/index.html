PSPA-Bench's LLM-Base scores 0.002 without the accessibility tree; every augmented agent still loses a third to a half of its score at high complexity

PSPA-Bench (arXiv:2603.29318, Northwestern Polytechnical University, Tsinghua University, Peking University and the Beijing Institute of Mathematical Sciences and Applications, submitted 31 March 2026) scores agents against 100 user personas and more than 12,855 personalized instructions across 22 apps. A bare LLM-Base agent, given no accessibility input at all, scores 0.002 on the paper's own 0-to-1 scale, essentially zero. Adding the accessibility tree lifts every one of the paper's 11 agent designs above 0.5, but by our own reading of its Table 2, moving a task from low to high complexity still costs each of those 11 designs between a third and a half of its score. phoneshell has never reported a success rate anywhere near 0.002: its worst recorded run on its own leaderboard is 55 of 58 scored tasks.

"PSPA-Bench: A Personalized Benchmark for Smartphone GUI Agent" (arXiv:2603.29318v1) builds personalization around a user profile the paper writes as ρu, which "induces a conditional distribution" over which instruction a given user would actually give. Each task is backed by a task decomposition graph with some steps fixed and some left open to preference, filled from slots like <product_category> or <price_range> that the agent has to resolve from the profile's long-term preferences ("favoring eco-friendly brands," in the paper's own example) or short-term signals such as recent searches. The paper does not say whether its agents read a stated profile directly or infer it from behavior; what it says is that personalization has to flow through the same decomposition graph that defines whether the task was completed at all, so getting the preference wrong and getting the task wrong are measured by the same structure, not two separate checks.

Eleven agent designs, scored by a ratio the paper never turns into a percentage

PSPA-Bench does not report success rate as a percentage of tasks completed. Its Table 2 scores every agent design on two ratio metrics, APR and PPR, each on a 0-to-1 scale; the text we read does not spell out what either initialism expands to, so we report them exactly as the paper labels them rather than guess. What the numbers show regardless of their full name: a bare LLM-Base agent, with no accessibility-tree input, scores 0.002 on APR at both low and high task complexity, functionally a zero. The moment any architecture adds accessibility-tree access, that score jumps by more than two orders of magnitude, into a 0.6-to-0.7 range at low complexity. The jump itself is the paper's cleanest result, cleaner than anything that follows it: without structured UI information, a personalized smartphone task is close to impossible for the agents tested, no matter how it is prompted.

What complexity costs, once the accessibility tree is already there

Strip out LLM-Base, whose near-zero score tells you about the accessibility tree rather than about complexity, and a second pattern holds across all ten remaining designs: every one of them loses APR score moving from low to high complexity, and by our own calculation from the table above, the relative loss sits in a fairly narrow band, from 32.7% for Mobile-Agent E (0.695 down to 0.468) to 48.3% for Self-Refine (0.596 down to 0.308). Architectural differences that separate the agents by as much as 0.1 at low complexity (0.596 to 0.695) compress at high complexity into a tighter cluster (0.308 to 0.468), which suggests complexity is eroding something closer to a shared bottleneck than to any one architecture's particular weakness. The paper's own instruction-generation validation, a human study comparing its generated instructions against random and static alternatives, reported 81.3% preference for its own method over random and 72.5% over static, which at least supports that the harder instructions are harder because of genuine preference ambiguity, not because the benchmark's own task generator produced a worse-written sentence.

What it means for reading a personalization claim

PSPA-Bench's own conclusion is blunt about where this leaves the field: "existing methods perform poorly under personalized settings, with even the best agent achieving limited success." The paper's limitations section adds two caveats worth repeating rather than smoothing over: its instructions are "synthetically generated based on predefined templates rather than collected from real users," and the benchmark is "primarily designed for Android devices." Whether its evaluation ran on physical hardware or an emulator is not stated in the sections we read, so we report that as not reported rather than assume either way.

phoneshell's own numbers live in a different register entirely: a 55-of-58 run on a physical iPhone, reported as a plain success count because every task it runs can be reduced to a pass or fail read from device state. PSPA-Bench cannot do that for personalization, because a personalized task does not have a single correct screen state to check against, it has a profile-dependent one, and that is a structurally harder grading problem than the one phoneshell's Check list was built to solve. The ratio metrics PSPA-Bench reports instead are a reasonable response to that harder problem, but they are not interchangeable with a success percentage, and a reader who converts 0.695 into "69.5% success" is making a translation the paper itself never makes.

What would prove this wrong

We claim PSPA-Bench's Table 2 reports an APR of 0.002 for LLM-Base at both complexity levels, and APR values ranging from 0.596 to 0.695 at low complexity and 0.308 to 0.468 at high complexity for its other ten agent designs, all checkable directly against the cited arXiv v1 HTML. The 32.7%-to-48.3% relative-drop range is our own calculation from those same printed values, shown in full above rather than asserted. This should be revisited if a later revision of the paper defines APR and PPR in full and those definitions change how the ratio should be read, or if any tested design holds its low-complexity score within 10 relative percent at high complexity, by 30 June 2027.
