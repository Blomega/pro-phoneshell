AnTrap set out to prove GUI agents are universally vulnerable to runtime anomalies; its own coordinate-offset trap raised three of fourteen models' scores

AnTrap (arXiv:2608.24099, submitted 25 August 2026) injects ten categories of runtime anomaly into 236 AndroidWorld-based tasks across 16 GUI agent models and reports, in its abstract, “universal vulnerability to dynamic anomalies.” Its own Table 3 shows that under Grounding Error, the one trap that most directly resembles a vision-grounding failure, three of the fourteen models with a tabulated score do not drop at all. They score higher than on the unperturbed task set.

Ten ways to break an agent, one of which moves the tap itself

AnTrap, full name "AnTrap: Evaluating Agents in Dynamic Adversarial Environments," comes from Guo Gan, Yilun Zhao, Cong Chen, Jinbiao Wei, Tingyu Song, Zheyuan Yang, Lin Fu, and Hong Zhou, across Zhejiang University, Yale University, the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Tongji University. Posted to arXiv on 25 August 2026, it builds a four-layer taxonomy, State, Thinking, Action, and Round, with ten subcategories: External Interruption and Visual Obscuration at the State layer; Temporal Conflict and Visual Hallucination at Thinking; Grounding Error, Type Mismatch, and Intent Deviation at Action; and State Deadlock, Context Disruption, and Loop at Round. The benchmark augments AndroidWorld's original 116 tasks to 236, scores agents with a rule-based success metric at Pass@3, and reports that a validated sample finds 91% of tasks satisfy its construction criteria. It evaluates 16 models from 7 organizations and concludes, in its abstract, that there is "universal vulnerability to dynamic anomalies" across them, though it also finds that "single-step traps at the state and action layers are largely addressable," while "deep contextual traps, like state deadlock," are not.

Grounding Error, the Action-layer trap, is built differently from most of the others: Section 3.3 states that the harness intercepts the agent's output "to dynamically override coordinates or action types before execution," so the tap the agent intended to make at its chosen target is redirected to a different point on the screen, while the model's own reasoning trace is left untouched. That makes it the one trap in the taxonomy that most directly resembles a real vision-grounding failure, where an agent correctly identifies an element but fails to place the tap accurately. Table 3 reports success rates under this trap for 14 of the 16 models (the two GPT-5.4 variants are excluded, per the paper's appendix, because their inference mode does not carry a grounding requirement). Three of those fourteen score higher under the trap than on the unperturbed task set.

An injected offset, not a measured natural failure

It matters that Grounding Error is constructed by overriding the executed action, not by observing where a model's own grounding goes wrong on its own. The trap changes only the coordinate that gets sent to the device; the model's stated target and reasoning are left exactly as it produced them. By that construction, every model facing this trap is, in effect, being tested on how much a single mis-executed tap costs its overall task success, not on whether it can locate elements correctly in the first place. The paper's companion result (Table 4) trains two models with GRPO directly against the trap set: UI-TARS-7B rises from a 17.8% base to 25.4% after trap-aware training, a 7.6-point gain the authors mark as a genuine, learnable improvement. That a model can be trained to recover from this specific injected offset is some evidence the trap carries a real, consistent signal; it does not explain why three untrained models would score higher under it than without it.

The spread across models, laid out side by side

Laid out model by model, the direction of the change under the Grounding Error trap is not predicted by how strong a model is unperturbed. The two highest-scoring models in this table, Claude-Sonnet-4.6 and Gemini-3-Pro, both drop by exactly 4.3 points. The weakest model in the table, UI-TARS-1.5-7B, takes by far the largest hit, -11.9 points. In between, three mid-tier models gain rather than lose.

What it means for a "universal vulnerability" claim

An abstract-level claim averaged across ten very different anomaly subcategories, spanning four layers from a dropped notification to a multi-turn state deadlock, can be true in aggregate while any one subcategory behaves inconsistently model to model. That is what AnTrap's own Table 3 shows for Grounding Error specifically: most models lose a few points, one loses nearly twelve, and three gain slightly. With 236 tasks split across ten subcategories and a Pass@3 metric, the per-category sample sizes are not stated in the sections available, so small-sample noise remains a plausible explanation for the three increases, one the paper itself does not address since it reports success rates without confidence intervals at the subcategory level. What the data does rule out is treating "universal vulnerability to dynamic anomalies" as meaning every model degrades under every trap; for this trap, a quarter of the tabulated models did not.

What would prove this wrong

We claim that AnTrap's Table 3 (arXiv:2608.24099v1) reports Claude-Sonnet-4.6 dropping from 74.2% to 69.9% and UI-TARS-1.5-7B dropping from 29.7% to 17.8% under the Grounding Error trap, while GUI-Owl-1.5-8B-Instruct rises from 64.8% to 65.7%, GUI-Owl-1.5-8B-Think rises from 66.1% to 67.4%, and Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking rises from 62.7% to 64.4%, all checkable directly against the cited arXiv v1 HTML. This should be revisited if the authors or a replication publish per-subcategory task counts large enough to rule out sampling noise and the three increases persist outside the margin, confirming a genuine robustness effect, or if revised or larger-scale data shows the increases disappear, confirming they were noise, by 31 December 2027.
