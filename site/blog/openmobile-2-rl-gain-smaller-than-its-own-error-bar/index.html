OpenMobile-2's reinforcement-learning step moves AndroidWorld accuracy less than its own reported error bar

OpenMobile-2 (arXiv:2610.14157, Nanjing University, SenseTime, and five other institutions, submitted 9 October 2026) trains mobile GUI agents through a staged recipe: a base model, GUI-only supervised fine-tuning, hybrid SFT, then reinforcement learning. On AndroidWorld Pass@1, the one metric in the paper's main results table that carries a reported error bar, the RL stage moves the 9B model from 73.3 plus or minus 1.5% to 74.0 plus or minus 2.9%, and the 27B model from 80.8 plus or minus 3.5% to 79.9 plus or minus 3.0%. Both changes are smaller than the paper's own reported spread.

The paper's 24 authors, led by Chenyang Yan, position OpenMobile-2 as "a near-frontier mobile agent with fully open training environments and recipes," releasing roughly 12,000 SFT trajectories, over 2,000 RL-ready executable tasks with automatic rewards, and a 35-app commercial-style simulator called MobileGym++ alongside the model weights. Its stated advances are open training data at scale and hybrid GUI-plus-tool-call action, not the RL stage specifically, which the paper applies after SFT as the final step in both its 9B and 27B model lines.

The clear gains are on the benchmark the authors built

Table 2 scores each training stage on eight metrics across four benchmarks: AndroidWorld (Pass@1 and Pass@3), MobileWorld (GUI and MCP variants), MobileGym (success rate SR and progress rate PR), MobileGym++ (GUI and Hybrid), and SPA-Bench L3, a real-device evaluation. Only the AndroidWorld Pass@1 column carries a plus-or-minus spread in the table; none of the other seven columns do. On that one column with a stated margin, RL's effect sits inside its own noise band for both model sizes. On MobileGym success rate, a benchmark inherited and extended by the same team rather than an independent one, RL's effect is unambiguous in direction for the 9B model (35.9% to 38.3%) and for the 27B model (48.4% to 51.2%), though no error bar is reported there to check whether those gaps clear the same kind of noise.

Why the gap lands where it lands

AndroidWorld is an external benchmark, built by a different team, with 116 tasks across 20 real applications the OpenMobile-2 authors did not design. MobileGym is partly their own: its Appendix describes MobileGym++ as combining "35 applications built in this work" with "28 inherited from MobileGym," the earlier benchmark from the same lineage. A training recipe tends to fit most cleanly to the data and reward signal closest to how it was built, and OpenMobile-2's over 2,000 RL-ready tasks with automatic rewards were constructed inside that same MobileGym++/MobileGym++ environment family, not inside AndroidWorld. That is an ordinary and expected pattern in agent training, not a flaw unique to this paper, but it means the RL stage's clearest measured win lands on the benchmark most similar to its own training distribution, while its effect on the outside benchmark does not clear the paper's own stated measurement noise.

What it means for reading a staged training recipe

A paper that reports a confidence interval on one metric and not on seven others is not hiding anything by doing so, every ablation table has to stop somewhere, but a reader evaluating a specific claim ("RL improves AndroidWorld performance") should use the column where uncertainty is actually quantified rather than the columns where a bare point estimate might look more decisive. Here the quantified column is the one that does not clear its own noise, and the larger, cleaner-looking deltas sit on the benchmark closest to the authors' own training environment, where no bar is given to check them against. Neither fact invalidates OpenMobile-2's broader contribution, the released training data and MobileGym++ environment are useful independent of this one metric, but it does mean the specific claim "RL improves AndroidWorld generalization" is not demonstrated by Table 2 as published; the claim "RL improves MobileGym performance" is directionally supported, pending a stated error bar for that column too.

What would prove this wrong

We claim Table 2 of arXiv:2610.14157v1 reports AndroidWorld Pass@1 for the 9B model at 73.3% ±1.5 before RL and 74.0% ±2.9 after, for the 27B model at 80.8% ±3.5 before RL and 79.9% ±3.0 after, and MobileGym success rate rising from 35.9% to 38.3% (9B) and 48.4% to 51.2% (27B) over the same stage, all checkable directly against the cited arXiv v1 HTML. This should be revisited if a later revision of the paper adds error bars to the MobileGym columns and those gaps also sit inside the noise, or if an independent reproduction reports an AndroidWorld gain from the same RL recipe that clears a properly measured confidence interval, by 30 June 2027.
