See-Control drives the phone with a robotic arm and a camera; hard single-app tasks succeed 3 times in 36

See-Control (arXiv:2512.08629, University College London, Imperial College London, Huawei's Noah's Ark Lab, ShanghaiTech University and the Southern University of Science and Technology, submitted 9 December 2025) operates a smartphone through a low-DoF robotic arm and screen-mirrored vision, with no ADB connection and no accessibility tree at all. On the paper's own 155-task ESO benchmark, hard single-app tasks succeed 3 times in 36, and cross-app tasks succeed 2 times in 15. The paper reports neither a baseline model nor an ablation study to explain the drop. phoneshell drives the same class of device entirely in software, grading every task from accessibility-level device state rather than a camera pointed at a screen.

"See-Control: A Multimodal Agent Framework for Smartphone Interaction with a Robotic Arm" describes a system built to operate a phone the way a person with motor limitations might: "via direct physical interaction with a low-DoF robotic arm," the paper's phrase, and explicitly "without requiring ADB or system back-end access." That constraint rules out the accessibility tree by construction, not by choice of baseline: there is no software bridge into the phone at all, only a camera watching a mirrored screen and an arm that taps, swipes, and types by moving in physical space. The vision stack pairs OCR for on-screen text with Grounding DINO for icons, and marks candidate targets with index-mark visual prompting the authors describe as "inspired by LLM-Optic and SoM," feeding a GPT-4o backbone that picks the next action from five primitives: Tap, Swipe, Text, Back, Exit.

155 tasks, one benchmark the authors built themselves, no comparison model

The paper's evaluation runs entirely on its own ESO (Embodied Smartphone Operation) benchmark: 140 single-app tasks and 15 cross-app tasks, split by difficulty into Simple, Medium, and Hard. AndroidWorld, the closest thing to a standard reference point for this kind of work, appears only in the paper's reference list, never as a comparison run. No other agent, framework, or baseline configuration of See-Control itself is scored against these 155 tasks; the numbers below are the entire quantitative result the paper reports.

A touch without a bridge, and what that removes from the agent

The mechanism here is the absence of a mechanism other mobile agents rely on. Where an accessibility-tree agent gets element names, types, and labels directly from the OS, See-Control's agent has to re-derive all of that from pixels, through OCR and an object detector, then convert a decision into a physical gesture a robotic arm executes against real glass: tap coordinates have to be correct not in screen space but in the arm's own coordinate frame, and the paper names "screen glare, touch friction, alignment errors" as the physical-world costs an emulator never has to pay. Text entry works by "keyboard-key localization from anchor points," meaning every character typed re-locates a key on the rendered keyboard from vision rather than sending a key code, a slower and more error-prone path than any software input method. The paper's own step-efficiency metric, HStep divided by AStep, is defined in a way that inverts the usual reading of such a ratio, which the paper does not flag as unusual; we note it rather than silently normalize it to match other papers' conventions.

What it means for reading a robotic-touch result

See-Control's conclusion frames the project modestly: Embodied Smartphone Operation agents "offer a complementary path, rather than a replacement, for MLLM-based smartphone operation agents," and the paper does not claim to outperform a software-connected agent anywhere in its text. That restraint is appropriate given what the numbers show: a single-digit-percent success rate on hard single-app tasks, no baseline model run on the same 155 tasks, and no ablation isolating whether the OCR stage, the detector, the backbone, or the arm's own positioning accounts for most of the failures. A reader cannot tell from this paper whether a better vision-language backbone would move the Hard-task numbers meaningfully, because nothing in the paper varies the backbone while holding the arm constant, or varies the arm's precision while holding the backbone constant.

The paper's real contribution is proving the mechanism works at all without a software bridge, which matters for accessibility use cases where ADB access or an accessibility-service grant is not available or not permitted. phoneshell's own leaderboard measures something adjacent but distinct: not whether a phone can be operated without any software access, but how well an agent performs when it has the best access iOS will grant it. Neither number substitutes for the other, and a comparison that put See-Control's 3-in-36 next to phoneshell's 55-of-58 without naming this gap would misstate what either number is for.

What would prove this wrong

We claim See-Control's Table 1 reports single-app Standard success of 11/13, 5/13, and 1/11 across Simple, Medium, and Hard difficulty, single-app Challenging success of 23/32, 18/35, and 3/36 across the same three levels, and cross-app success of 2/15 overall, all checkable directly against the cited arXiv v1 HTML, which also contains no baseline model and no ablation study over its own pipeline stages. This should be revisited if a later revision of the paper adds either, or if a follow-up paper runs the same ESO benchmark with a software-connected (ADB or accessibility-tree) agent for direct comparison, by 30 June 2027.
