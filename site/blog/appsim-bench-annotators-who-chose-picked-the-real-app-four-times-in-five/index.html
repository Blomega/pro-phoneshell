AppSim-Bench calls its 17 simulated apps high-fidelity; when its annotators chose, they picked the real app four times in five

In AppSim-Bench's own blind A/B study, annotators who made a choice picked the simulated app as the real one in 19.0% of static-screenshot cases and 15.2% of dynamic ones (arXiv:2609.07712, submitted 7 September 2026). Two apps nobody can tell apart would land near 50%. The paper reads the study as demonstrating "high visual fidelity," and it contains no measurement of whether an agent scores the same on a simulated app as on the real app it copies.

"AppSim-Bench: Bridging Real-world Apps and Reproducible Evaluation for Mobile GUI Agents" comes from ten authors at Central China Normal University and Acrab AI's Agentic Labs, with Jintian Feng first and Yuxuan Shi corresponding. It rebuilds task-relevant flows of 17 apps (9 Chinese, including WeChat, JD and Amap; 8 English, including Amazon, Instagram and Uber Eats) as native Android apps with resettable backends, and defines 557 tasks graded by deterministic state and answer checks with no LLM in the scoring stage. Across 19 agents the best, Claude-Opus-4.7, completes 50.27%, and 28.55% of tasks are solved by no agent.

The realism study has a large undecided share and a lopsided decided share

Section 4.2 describes the study: "seven annotators conducted a blind A/B study over 11 sessions." For static screenshots there were 510 judgments, 46.3% of them "undecided." Among the 274 decided, the simulation was picked as real in 19.0%. For dynamic sequences there were 255 judgments, 35.7% undecided, and the simulation was picked as real in 15.2% of the 164 decided. The paper's reading: "The study demonstrates the high visual fidelity of our simulated applications."

Both readings are in the data. Close to half of static comparisons left annotators unable to choose, which is real evidence of resemblance. When they did choose, they were right about 81% of the time on screenshots and about 85% on sequences. Converting the paper's percentages into shares of all judgments (our arithmetic, rounding to whole judgments): for screenshots, 43.5% picked the real app, 10.2% picked the simulation, 46.3% were undecided; for sequences, 54.5%, 9.8% and 35.7%. Adding motion moved 10.6 points out of "undecided," and nearly all of it went to the correct answer.

Looking alike to a person is a different property from scoring alike for an agent

A benchmark built on simulated apps makes an implicit claim: an agent's score here predicts its score on the real apps. Testing that needs the same agents run on matched tasks in both places. AppSim-Bench does not report that. Its limitations section says so in its own terms: "Behavioral fidelity is measured indirectly, through the placeholder residual," and it adds that complementary real-world studies are still needed.

The placeholder residual is the closest thing to a behavioural check. Because the apps "reproduce task-relevant UI flows" and prune the rest, an agent can wander into a "page under development" screen. An audit of 6 apps, 3 agents and 218 tasks (654 executions) found 28 executions that entered a placeholder and still passed, which is 4.28%. Section 4.2 describes this as accounting for "at most 4.28% of measured success," while Appendix E gives 4.28% as a share of all 654 executions. Those are different denominators. With the audited agents passing well under 100% of executions, 28 placeholder passes are a larger share of successes than 4.28%; the paper does not print the number of successes in the audit, so the exact share is not reported.

One result in Table 2 hints at where simulated and real might diverge. The top four models each score 18 to 21 points higher on the Chinese apps than the English ones (Claude-Opus-4.7: 60.07% against 40.15%), and Doubao-Seed-1.6 has the widest gap at 50.88% against 27.01%. That could be the tasks, the apps, or how each set was rebuilt. Without a real-app arm there is no way to separate them.

What it means for choosing between a simulator and a device

The trade is plain. AppSim-Bench gets 557 resettable tasks, deterministic grading and 19 agents at temperature 0 with a 50-step cap, none of which is practical against live WeChat or Uber Eats with real accounts. What it gives up is the ability to say that 50.27% means anything about the real WeChat. Other simulator papers have paid for that claim directly: MobileGym ran a real-device subset, which we covered in sim-trained gains keep 95 percent on a real phone, and we noted the same missing number in ScaleWoB.

Our judgement: the minimum credible fidelity check for a simulated-app benchmark is a rank correlation across agents on a matched subset, even 30 tasks in 3 apps. A visual A/B study answers a question about human perception, and here its decided cases point the other way from the adjective attached to it.

phoneshell makes the opposite trade and has the opposite weakness. The 80 public tasks run on one physical iPhone 17 Pro Max on iOS 26.6, against the real apps, graded from device state. The simulator path exists, and phoneshell/cli.py labels it in one line: "same thing against a simulator, for development." That removes the fidelity question and costs parallelism and reset speed: one phone, 80 public tasks.

What would prove this wrong

We claim arXiv:2609.07712v1 reports the simulation picked as real in 19.0% of 274 decided static judgments and 15.2% of 164 decided dynamic judgments, and reports no agent score on a real app. Our prediction: if a matched sim-versus-real comparison on AppSim-Bench apps is published by 30 June 2027, per-app success for at least one of the top four models will differ by more than 10 points between the simulated and real version. A matched study showing every top-four model within 5 points on every tested app would prove the simulators behaviourally faithful and this note too cautious.
