AndroidDaily's best score is 62.0% on 350 real tasks; phoneshell catches one of its three failure bottlenecks and none of a model that mis-sees a correct screen

AndroidDaily (arXiv:2605.27761, StepFun, Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications and Waseda University, accepted to ACM Multimedia 2026) runs 350 real daily-use tasks across 94 closed-source Android apps and finds the strongest of twelve evaluated models, Gemini 3 Flash, succeeds 62.0% of the time. Its manual failure analysis names three dominant bottlenecks: inference latency, memory-induced action loops, and a model's own action protocol degrading perception it otherwise has. phoneshell's harness built a direct countermeasure for the second of those, a partial one for the first, and has never had to answer the third, because nothing in it watches for a model that mis-sees a screen it could otherwise read correctly.

The paper, "AndroidDaily: A Verifiable Benchmark for Mobile GUI Agents on Real-World Closed-Source Applications," was posted to arXiv on 26 May 2026 by a team from StepFun, BUPT and Waseda and is scheduled for ACM Multimedia 2026 in Rio de Janeiro this November. Its central contribution is a judge, GRADE, built to replace human review at scale: evaluated against 879 manually reviewed sessions, the full two-layer version of GRADE reaches 87.37% agreement with human evaluators, up from 84.76% for its evidence layer alone. Every task caps a session at 60 interaction steps and 40 minutes of wall-clock time, scored pass@1. The task set splits into Information and Decision (173 tasks, 49.4%), Execution and Operations (78 tasks, 22.3%), and Creation and Communication (99 tasks, 28.3%).

The fastest model per step, Step-GUI-30A3 at 2.0 seconds, is not close to the most successful; the slowest, Gemini 3 Pro at 30.0 seconds, loses to its own faster sibling Gemini 3 Flash by 3.4 points. Speed and success move independently here, which is exactly what makes the paper's failure analysis worth reading past the leaderboard: a model that fails fast and a model that fails slow are not failing for the same reason.

Three bottlenecks, and what each one looks like on screen

Manually categorizing failed sessions from the top three frontier and top three GUI-specialized models, the authors write that "our failure analysis identifies three dominant bottlenecks: inference latency causing UI misalignment, memory-induced action loops, and protocol-induced capability degradation." The paper gives each a concrete shape rather than leaving it abstract.

Latency shows up two ways. The slow one is an accumulated-time failure: "accumulated per-step latency frequently pushes sessions past the 40-minute wall-clock limit." The sharp one is a race condition: "during the delay between decision and execution, a transient UI element" appears and "shifts the intended click target," so "the tap lands on the wrong element, forcing the agent to dismiss it and navigate back." Memory-induced loops are simpler to state: "the agent loses track of its history and cycles between a few states indefinitely, exhausting the step budget without progress." Protocol-induced degradation is the strangest of the three, because nothing about the screen or the model's own visual competence has changed: the paper's case study has a model correctly identify, under a plain visual question, that "pizza" is absent from a screen, then, "under the agent protocol," hallucinate a click on a visually similar item anyway. The authors describe the structured action protocol itself as something that "can probabilistically interfere with the underlying VLM's visual reasoning," concluding that "the perceptual capability exists but fails to activate under the structured action protocol." The paper reports no percentage breakdown across the three categories; that figure is, in its own words, not reported.

phoneshell has two pieces of code built for exactly two of these shapes, for reasons that predate AndroidDaily entirely. agent/verify.py's StuckDetector keeps the last eight screen signatures and actions and flags two patterns: the same action repeated with no screen change, and an A-B-A-B alternation across six consecutive states, "this is a loop." That is memory-induced looping, named and caught, independent of any particular model's own memory failing to notice it. The same file's consistency_gate runs before every tap: it crops the claimed element out of the current frame and checks, via OCR, whether the pixels there actually say what the accessibility tree claims. Its own docstring states the reason bluntly: "a stale or wrong label overrides correct visual perception 30-79% of the time in measurement, and that becomes a wrong action 65-100% of the time." That is a direct answer to AndroidDaily's latency-driven misalignment, the case where the screen moved during the gap between a model's decision and its execution, catching it at the moment of action rather than trying to make the model faster.

What it means for anyone building the harness around a model, not the model itself

  • Two of AndroidDaily's three bottlenecks are things a harness sitting between a model and a phone can catch without touching the model at all. phoneshell's two defenses here were not written in response to this paper, they predate it, which is itself evidence that these failure shapes are generic enough to show up independently in two different research efforts aimed at the same problem.
  • The third bottleneck is not a harness problem. "Protocol-induced capability degradation" is, by the paper's own account, the agent's action-protocol prompting suppressing a visual competence the same model demonstrates it has under a plain VQA query. There is nothing for an external observer checking pixels against accessibility labels to catch, because the model's perception of the correct pixels was never wrong; only its action under the protocol was. Fixing it means changing the protocol, not adding a gate downstream of it.
  • The paper's own proposed mitigations split cleanly along this same line: "latency-aware agent design" and long-horizon memory that does not degrade across many steps are the kind of fix a harness or a training run can deliver externally, while "capability-preserving agentic protocols" is a claim about the prompting and action schema itself, something no amount of post-hoc verification on the harness side will touch.

What would prove this wrong

We claim AndroidDaily's Table 2 puts Gemini 3 Flash's overall success at 62.0% against 350 tasks across 94 apps, and that its Section 4.4 names the three bottlenecks quoted above with no accompanying percentage breakdown; both are checkable against the arXiv HTML directly and would be false if a later revision changes the table or adds the missing breakdown. We also claim no file in phoneshell's codebase, as of 6 November 2026, contains any check for a model's action-protocol prompting degrading its own stated perceptual accuracy; that is checkable by grepping the repository. This should be revisited if phoneshell adds such a check, or if AndroidDaily's camera-ready ACM Multimedia version, due this November, reports the per-category breakdown the preprint does not, by 31 March 2027.
