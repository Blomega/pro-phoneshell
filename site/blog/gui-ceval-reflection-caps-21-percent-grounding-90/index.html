GUI-CEval runs entirely on real phones; the same models that tap the right spot 90% of the time catch their own mistakes at most 21% of the time

GUI-CEval (arXiv:2603.15039, accepted CVPR 2026) says it is “built entirely on physical device environments”: 201 mainstream Chinese apps across four real mobile device types, 4,028 agent tasks, 20 models and multi-agent systems scored across five dimensions. The same models that locate the right on-screen element to tap (Grounding) up to 90.10% of the time, and perceive screen content (Perception) up to 82.28% of the time, catch their own mistakes after acting (Reflection) at most 21.01% of the time. Chained end to end into a real on-device task, the best success rate is 33.33%.

Five dimensions, scored on real devices, not a simulator

GUI-CEval, from Yang Li and ten coauthors at the HyperAI Team, Xiaomi Corporation, was submitted to arXiv on 16 March 2026 and accepted at CVPR 2026. The paper's device claim is repeated across the text rather than appearing once: the abstract says the benchmark is "built entirely on physical device environments," the introduction says data collection spans "four real mobile device types" and that "all data are human-curated on real mobile devices and verified," and Section 3.2 describes "a device pool spanning various screen sizes and resolutions, including smartphones, tablets, and foldable devices," with a custom tool capturing interactions "directly on real devices." The paper does not name the specific device models, their exact count, or state outright that none of the four device types is virtual, so that detail should be read as the authors' description rather than an independently verified device list.

Within that device pool, the benchmark spans 201 mainstream Chinese apps, with 4,028 agent tasks and 4,194 multimodal question-answering tasks, and evaluates 20 representative models and multi-agent systems across 47 configurations. Five scored dimensions sit underneath the headline numbers: Perception, Planning, Reflection, Evaluation, and Grounding, plus two end-to-end task metrics, Offline Agent and Online Agent, which chain the dimensions together on a live device. Section 4.1 draws the contrast directly: "perception-related scores such as Perception and Grounding remain high (peaking at 82.28% and 90.10%, respectively)," while "reasoning and correction-related metrics (Reflection and Evaluation) lag far behind (maximums of 21.01% and 49.24%)." The same section's stated conclusion: "Strong perception does not guarantee reliable execution."

Why perception and grounding do not carry over to reflection

Reflection and Evaluation are scored differently from Perception and Grounding, and that difference is part of why the gap exists. Section 3.1.1 states that "all tasks adopt a unified prompt format requiring a single choice, with accuracy as the primary metric": for every one of the five dimensions, a model answers a multiple-choice question about a captured screen or trajectory, rather than performing live self-correction mid-task. Reflection asks a model to judge, after the fact, whether a prior action in a trajectory succeeded or should be redone; Evaluation asks it to judge whether a full trajectory satisfied the task. Perception and Grounding ask more direct questions: what is on the screen, and where is the element to tap. Section 4.2 adds that Reflection and Evaluation are "the least sensitive to resolution," meaning the gap is not primarily an artifact of screen size or image quality, the variable the paper's physical device pool was built to vary.

Two different "real device" setups

GUI-CEval's physical-device claim and phoneshell's are not the same shape of claim. GUI-CEval pools many unnamed devices across four types to build a large, static dataset of 4,028 tasks and 4,194 QA items, scored offline against that captured data for four of its five dimensions, with only the Online Agent metric executing live via ADB. phoneshell runs every graded task live, on one named, specific device, and never scores from a captured snapshot.

What it means for a real-device benchmark

Running on real phones, tablets, and foldables instead of a simulator settles one question (does the input look like a real screen) without settling another (can the model tell, afterward, whether its own action worked). GUI-CEval's data shows those as separable: the best Grounding and Perception scores sit close to the ceiling, and the best Reflection score sits well under a quarter. For anyone grading an agent from its own self-report, that gap matters directly, because the dimension a self-reporting agent relies on most, judging whether it succeeded, is the one GUI-CEval finds weakest across all 20 models tested, including the frontier-adjacent Qwen2.5-VL-72B. A device-state check sidesteps the question of whether the model can reflect correctly by never asking it to; it reads what changed on the device instead of asking the model what it thinks changed.

What would prove this wrong

We claim that GUI-CEval (arXiv:2603.15039v1, Table 2, Section 4.1) reports a maximum Perception score of 82.28% and a maximum Grounding score of 90.10%, against a maximum Reflection score of 21.01% and a maximum Evaluation score of 49.24%, with a maximum end-to-end Online Agent success rate of 33.33%, all checkable directly against the cited arXiv v1 HTML. This should be revisited if a later release of GUI-CEval, or a comparably scaled real-device Chinese-app benchmark, reports any single model's Reflection score at or above 40%, roughly double the current best, by 31 December 2027.
