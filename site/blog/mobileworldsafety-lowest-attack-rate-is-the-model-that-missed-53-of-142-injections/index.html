MobileWorldSafety's lowest attack rate belongs to the model that never reached the injection in 53 of 142 runs

MobileWorldSafety (arXiv:2608.17659, submitted 18 August 2026) reports "attack success rates ranging from 40.4% to 66.9%" for six mobile agents on 142 injection tasks. The 40.4% is MAI-UI-8B, which failed to reach the injected content in 53 of its 142 runs. The 66.9% is Gemini-3-Pro, which failed to reach it in 12. Attack success is computed only over the runs that got there, so the two rates are measured on 89 and 130 tasks, and the paper gives no rate on the tasks both reached.

The paper is by Sujin Chen, Lijun Li, Tianyi Du and Jing Shao of Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. It builds on MobileWorld (arXiv:2512.19432) and its Dockerized Android emulators: 13 apps, 5 MCP servers, 142 tasks in which a message, web page, community post, stored record or tool response carries an instruction the user did not give. A state verifier flags completed attacks, and an LLM judge labels the ambiguous runs as partial, stalled or run failed. Every setting was run once: "we do not conduct multiple runs for the same experimental setting."

The published rate drops the runs that never met the attack, and that count varies by a factor of four

Section 3.4 defines the metric. Run Failed means "the agent never reaches the injection carrier due to capability limitations." Those runs are removed: the authors "exclude them from the evaluation base to prevent misjudging inadequate capability as agent safety." Attack success rate is then Executed plus Partial over what remains, which the paper calls Scorable.

Excluding those runs is the right call. Counting them as safe would make MAI-UI-8B look far better: its 36 attacked runs over all 142 tasks is 25.4%, against 61.3% for Gemini-3-Pro. The published 40.4% already corrects for that.

The correction creates a second problem that the paper does not address. The six rates sit on six different task subsets: 130, 124, 118, 120, 106 and 89 runs. The ordering of those subsets nearly reproduces the ordering of the result. The Spearman rank correlation between scorable count and ASR across the six models is 0.94 (our calculation); only Kimi-K2.5 and Claude-Sonnet-4.5 swap. Two readings fit that. More capable agents may be more willing to follow an injected instruction. Or the tasks a weaker agent manages to reach are the shallow ones, and shallow tasks carry easier-to-resist injections. The paper has the per-task labels to separate these by reporting ASR on the intersection of tasks that all six models reached. It does not.

Where each model's 142 runs went

The counts show how thin some comparisons are. Claude-Sonnet-4.5 has the lowest rate among the four general models at 47.5%, built from 40 Executed and 17 Partial. Partial is assigned only by the LLM judge. Count Executed alone, the label the state verifier assigns, and Claude drops to 33.3% while GUI-Owl-1.5-32B-Instruct sits at 36.8%: the two trade places. The judge was checked against humans on 76 ambiguous runs with 92.1% agreement (70 of 76), and on Gemini-3-Pro the humans marked 14 runs as run failed where the judge marked 12, which would move its denominator from 130 to 128.

The defense experiment has the same shape. A defensive system prompt cuts Qwen3.5's rate from 51.6% to 37.0%.

Executed falls by 17, from 59 to 42. Stalled rises by 18, from 2 to 20. Defended, the outcome where the attack is resisted and the user's task still gets done, moves from 58 to 60. The task completion rate goes from 46.8% to 47.2%. The paper says this plainly: the defense "appears to primarily trigger conservative refusal or abort behavior." A reader who sees only the ASR column sees a 14.6-point safety gain. The counts say the prompt converted completed attacks into abandoned tasks almost one for one.

What it means for excluding runs from a denominator

We exclude runs too. phoneshell reports a task that was never attempted as not scored, and phoneshell/bench/runner.py says so: "Skipped tasks are excluded entirely rather than counted as failures." The difference is the cause of the exclusion. Ours are rig faults and provider limits: the phone would not wake, setup failed, the model returned no answer under a usage cap. Those causes are not properties of the agent's skill at the task, so removing them does not bias a comparison between agents much. MobileWorldSafety's exclusion is caused by the agent under test. Conditioning a rate on something the agent did makes the remaining sample depend on the agent, and then the rates are not comparable without a common subset.

The rule we take from this table: an exclusion that depends on the system being measured needs the intersection reported next to it. With 142 tasks and 89 reached by the weakest model, the intersection is at most 89 tasks and probably fewer, which is still enough to rank six models once.

On the safety question itself we have no number to offer. The guard in phoneshell/safety.py states its threat model as "not a malicious model" and asks for confirmation when a tap target matches a pattern for irreversible actions. That would interrupt some Executed outcomes at the last tap and has never been tested against injected content. An injection that ends in an ordinary-looking Send would pass it.

What would prove this wrong

We claim that the 26.5-point spread in Table 1 of arXiv:2608.17659v1 (66.9% to 40.4%) is partly produced by each model being scored on a different subset of tasks. Our prediction: if the authors or anyone using their released task set publish ASR on the tasks scorable for all six models by 30 June 2027, the spread between Gemini-3-Pro and MAI-UI-8B will be smaller than 26.5 points. A common-subset spread of 26.5 points or more would show the denominator is not what separates them.
