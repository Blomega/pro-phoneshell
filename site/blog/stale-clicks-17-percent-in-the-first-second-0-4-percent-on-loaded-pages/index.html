17% of clicks planned in a page's first second go stale, 0.4% after it loads, and the one wait rule under 1% is one phoneshell cannot run

On 31 public web pages, 0.4% of clicks planned on a fully loaded page were stale 30 seconds later, against 17% of clicks planned in the first second after the page opened (arXiv:2610.39282v1, dated 29 October 2026). Seven wait rules were tested before the screenshot. One got under 1%: network idle, 0.46% at a median 1.24 s. phoneshell settles on pixel stability, the family that left 1.8% to 2.8%, and an iPhone gives it no network signal to switch to.

The paper is "What Does "Now" Mean to Your Computer-Use Agent?" by Vasundra Srinivasan, a single-author preprint with fourteen preregistered hypotheses, eight of them reported as refuted. It tracks up to 20 visible links and buttons per visit and asks whether a click at each planned position still reaches its element. A click is stale when the target "has moved, disappeared or been covered." The work it answers, PUSV (arXiv:2604.18860), measured a 6.5-second gap between screenshot and click and treated that gap as the risk. Section 5.1 moves the risk: the stale share on loaded pages was flat "at every interval from 0.5 to 30 s", and after a fresh open it reached 14.5% by 0.5 s and 17% by 1 s, then stopped rising.

The abstract says eight of ten agents' rules leave more than 1% stale. By point estimate the table shows 9: magentic-ui sits at 1.2% with an interval of 0.7 to 1.9. Eight is the count whose whole interval is above 1% (our reading; the paper does not say which it means). Agent S3 and UI-TARS-desktop both sleep one second and leave 11.7% and 9.1%, against 3.1% for the paper's own 1 s rule: 3.8 times worse for Agent S3 on the same nominal wait (our arithmetic). Section 5.1 gives the cause. Their delay "starts when the server first responds, so 7% of their screenshots are captured before the HTML has been parsed." Skyvern is the one agent that matches network idle, at 0.46%, by stacking the load event, about a second, and an animation wait.

Two limits on this table. These are rules replayed by the author, so nothing here is an end-to-end agent result. And the 31 pages are clocks, counters, maps, reference and status pages. On commercial pages the network-idle rule itself stops working: in the HTTP Archive crawl of October 2026, 15% of 65 desktop booking and buying home pages went quiet within 3 s, and 52% shifted layout after a capped wait, against 7.8% of 3,028 .gov pages (Table 2). On mobile home pages that gap shrank to 12.9 points and "vanished among fully recorded pages (27% against 28%)". The crawl's shift signal also failed its own transfer check against measured stale clicks (Spearman -0.12), so the commercial stale-click rate is, in the paper's words, "an unvalidated proxy."

phoneshell's step has the pixel wait and the after-check, and nothing between the plan and the tap

Phone.snapshot() in phoneshell/actions.py states its choice: "so we settle on pixels and read the tree once." wait_until_stable in phoneshell/perception/screen.py polls a screenshot every 0.3 s (0.12 s on screens seen before), allows 1.2% of pixels to differ, returns on the first quiet pair, and gives up at 3.5 s (1.4 s on the fast path). That is a visual-stability rule with a shorter quiet window than the paper's 0.5 s. A framebuffer diff does see the container fills and overlays that the browser's layout-shift signal misses, so the 2.8% and 1.8% figures do not transfer to it directly. What transfers is the shape of the failure: content that arrives after a quiet 0.3 s is invisible to any rule that only watches for motion, and only the network rule knew more was coming. WebDriverAgent exposes no request activity for the app under test, so phoneshell has no equivalent to switch to.

After the model answers, tap_element sends self.wda.tap_w3c(x, y) with coordinates from that snapshot. Its docstring argues the coordinate is "exactly what the model intended." The paper's airline example is that argument failing: the plan was right for the screenshot, a late banner pushed the form down 52 pixels, and the click landed on a different control. phoneshell runs no check of the target before the tap. It does run the cheap check afterwards: _after sleeps 0.6 s and compares one screenshot, a pixel change score. Table 3 has that kind of check detecting 90% of failed actions with no false alarms, and a 4B model detecting 100%.

A 312 ms pixel check pays above 10% stale for an ordinary click and above 1.2% for an irreversible one

Equation 1 gives the stale rate above which a pre-click check saves time: (check latency / planning time + false-alarm rate) / (recall x (k - 1) + false-alarm rate), with k the cost of a stale click in planning cycles. Fed the pixel check's Table 3 row (82% detected, 4% false alarms, 312 ms) and a 6.5 s planner, it returns 10.2% for an ordinary click (k = 2) and 1.2% for an irreversible one (k = 10). We recomputed both and they match the paper's "about 10%" and 1.2%. Claude Opus 5.5 as the checker (83%, 12%, 2,366 ms, 7.6 times the latency) returns 50.9%: it never pays for an ordinary click.

For a phone harness that sets the decision. At a loaded-page rate of 0.4% no check pays, and adding one to every tap would be waste. After an app launch or a screen push, the paper's 14% to 17% is above the ordinary-click threshold, if iOS screens behave like those web pages, which nobody has measured. Before a tap that phoneshell/safety.py already classes as irreversible, the threshold is 1.2%, and the patch comparison costs less than one of the settle probes phoneshell already runs. Our judgement: the pre-tap check belongs on the confirm-gated taps first, and on the first tap after a transition second.

The third rule is the one to copy without measuring anything. When a check fires, plan again from a new screenshot. Adjusting the click was unsafe in every configuration tested: the best pixel setting mis-adjusted 11% of the time and Claude Haiku 5.5 clicked wrongly 34% of the time.

What would prove this wrong

We claim phoneshell's pixel-settle rule leaves more than 1% of planned taps stale on a freshly opened screen. That is an extrapolation from desktop web pages to iOS apps, and the paper's own mobile crawl numbers warn against assuming it. The test: on the physical iPhone, launch each of the bench apps, take Phone.snapshot(), and for 6.5 s track whether each of up to 20 visible elements still sits under its planned coordinate, three rounds. If that measurement, run by us or anyone by 31 March 2027, puts the stale share at or under 1%, the pre-tap check is not worth building for ordinary taps and this note's recommendation is wrong.
