MobileProbe's 85.1% attack success is a search of up to 15 live runs per task, and better injection points explain at most 8.5 points of it

MobileProbe (arXiv:2611.16693v1, 12 November 2026, accepted to NDSS 2027) reports that its injected payloads hijack three mobile agents 85.1% to 86.3% of the time. That figure counts a task as broken when one payload succeeds anywhere inside a budget of 15 live runs, and the paper never replays the winning payload. Its framing is coverage: fixed injection points let agents dodge the attack. On Gemini-3.1-Pro, better placement recovers 8.5 points of exposure; the other 78.3 points between a static template and MobileProbe come from searching payloads against the live agent.

The paper is from The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Zhejiang University and Huawei. It builds 83 attack instances on MobileWorld base tasks across 12 apps: 44 Malicious Text (adversarial text overlaid on an email, message, post or product page) and 39 In-App Fake Dialog (a dialog drawn by a repackaged app). Goals are labelled Confidentiality (27), Integrity (28) and Availability (28). The targets are Gemini-3.1-Pro end to end, Gemini-3.1-Pro planning with UI-Ins-7B grounding, and the on-device GUI-Owl-1.5-8B-Instruct, all on Android Virtual Devices at 1080 x 2400. Payloads are written by Seed-2.0-Pro and judged by GPT-5.4.

The abstract's motivation is that "an agent that never reaches the injected page is recorded as safe without being tested." Table II measures that problem. With an expert choosing the injection point, Gemini-3.1-Pro missed the payload on 13.3% of tasks, the agentic pair on 12.0%, GUI-Owl on 22.9%. Choosing the point from each agent's own benign trajectory cut the misses to 4.8%, 3.6% and 6.0%. That is 8.5, 8.4 and 16.9 points of extra exposure (our arithmetic), and it is the most that coverage alone can add to an attack success rate.

Table III then holds the injection point constant: "All methods share the injection points selected by MobileProbe, isolating the effect of payload generation capability." Under identical placement, a retrieved template from AgentHazard or GhostEI-Bench reached 6.8% on Gemini and a one-call zero-shot payload 8.0%. MobileProbe reached 85.1%. The fixed-point benchmarks the paper criticises are therefore low for a second reason it states but does not separate in its headline: one hand-written payload per task, never adapted to how the agent reacted.

The success event is the first verified hit in a loop of up to 15 live runs

All three iterative methods "share the same per-task evaluation budget of" 15 live runs, and MobileProbe's loop runs "until attack success is confirmed or the evaluation budget is exhausted." The zero-shot baseline "generates one payload in a single LLM call, without execution feedback or iterative refinement." So the 85.1% versus 8.0% comparison is up to 15 tries against one. Table VI shows how much of the result the retries carry: with evolutionary refinement removed, Gemini ASR falls to 28.5%, a drop of 56.6 points (our arithmetic), and the full system used 7.9 live runs per task on average.

Two more properties of the score matter to anyone reusing it. First, the endpoints differ by vector. For a fake dialog, success is "whether the agent clicks the Accept button," read by the monitoring APK; no downstream harm is needed. For malicious text, a deterministic program checks device state, but "trajectory-based LLM judgment serves as a supplementary criterion" when the agent's actions align with the attack and the final state is not reached. The paper does not report how many successes the judge decided. The vector gap it reports (88.9% to 95.7% for dialogs, 78.0% to 81.8% for text) compares a one-tap endpoint with a state-change endpoint partly graded by a model.

Second, Availability success on text instances is verified by re-running MobileWorld's base-task check, so the attack wins when the original task fails. The three agents fail 45.4%, 43.4% and 73.5% of tasks with no attack at all (our arithmetic from benign utility of 54.6%, 56.6% and 26.5%). Availability is still the lowest-scoring goal, 75.0% to 81.0%, so the verifier is clearly not a free pass, but the paper does not say how an attack-caused failure is told apart from an ordinary one. That matters for its ASR-F claim, which it says confirms "genuine adversarial manipulation rather than inherent task difficulty." Splitting the overall rate by benign outcome, ASR on tasks the agents can complete comes out at about 80.8%, 77.9% and 78.7% (our arithmetic, assuming benign utility partitions the same task set).

A device-graded score should state its retry budget, its endpoint and its replay rate

The finding we take from Table III is real and useful: a payload adapted to the agent's own trace beats a hand-written one by roughly ten times on frontier agents, and GUI-Owl falls to templates at 34.5% where Gemini stays at 6.8%. Anyone publishing a mobile prompt-injection number with one static payload per task is reporting a floor.

The number to quote from MobileProbe, though, is an existence rate: for 85.1% of tasks a payload was found within 15 live runs. The threat model says the deployed attack is the static payload alone, "requiring no trajectory-sensing capabilities." The paper also says the same agent "may follow different operational paths across repeated runs" and that this caused residual misses. Neither statement is tested against the other. A replay of each winning payload on fresh runs would give the per-encounter compromise rate, which is the figure a defender budgets against. Our judgement: until that replay exists, compare MobileProbe numbers with other benchmarks only alongside the budget, written as success within 15 runs.

For phoneshell the dialog result lands on a specific line. Guard.classify_tap in phoneshell/safety.py pauses for confirmation when an element label matches one of 14 regexes in phoneshell/config.py (place order, pay, buy, transfer, delete, log out and others). We tested the labels "Accept", "Send", "Confirm", "Allow" and "OK" against those patterns and none matches. The module's own docstring sets the threat model as "not a malicious model, it is an ordinary one that misreads a screen." MobileProbe's four case studies (an SMS to an attacker number, an extra filter keyword, a doubled order quantity, a tracking code sent to the wrong account) would pass that gate, because the harmful step is a send or an accept rather than a payment. The WebDriverAgent setting "defaultAlertAction": "" in phoneshell/config.py keeps system alerts from being auto-accepted, but a fake dialog drawn by an app is app content, not a system alert.

What would prove this wrong

We predict that when the winning MobileProbe payloads are replayed unchanged on fresh runs of the same agent, the per-run success rate will be below 70% for Gemini-3.1-Pro, at least 15 points under the 85.1% search figure. If the authors or anyone else publish a replay of the 83 instances by 30 June 2027 (NDSS 2027 runs 22 to 26 March) showing a per-run rate of 70% or higher, the budget caveat in this note is overstated and the 85.1% can be read as a deployment rate.
