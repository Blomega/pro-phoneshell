nanoMuse gives its personal agent hands on Android but not on the iPhone; phoneshell's iPhone hands come from a Mac, and its gate misses two steps nanoMuse's catches

An open personal agent that runs on every device stops short at one of them. The iPhone gets the conversation and not the hands. phoneshell is the other answer, hands from outside the phone, and the two projects' safety rules can be compared step by step.

nanoMuse, an open-source personal agent posted on 6 October 2026, runs on Android, iOS, the desktop and the web. It has hands on every one of them except the iPhone, "since iOS lets no app operate another". phoneshell puts the hands outside the phone. A Mac drives an XCUITest runner over a cable, and six frontier models passed 322 of 349 device-graded iPhone runs through it. The two projects also gate dangerous steps differently. nanoMuse's Sentinel stops a tap on Send and a typing step that presses return. phoneshell's confirm gate stops neither.

What nanoMuse shipped

Guangyi Liu, Yong Liu and Jiangning Zhang (Zhejiang University; arXiv:2610.08699) describe nanoMuse as the open counterpart to Meta's Muse, under GPL-3.0. The first release came on 25 September, the report describes v0.1.40, and v0.1.41 followed on 7 October. The Android app is a modified OpenMinis with a sandboxed Alpine Linux and a shell. The desktop app is an Electron shell. The iOS app ships through TestFlight. Hands are the last rung of a ladder: skill or CLI or MCP server first, then a page fetched with the person's login, then the browser, then the device screen, off by default. The screen rung is a one-action-per-screenshot loop: the model returns a thought, a sentence saying what it is about to press, and one gesture.

The report is plain about two gaps. On the iPhone, Table 1's note says, "no agent has a phone screen there". On every device it says: "We have no success rate and no count of human take-overs to report." The Sentinel is described as a policy boundary in the same trust domain as the agent, not a privilege boundary.

How the hands reach an iPhone from outside

iOS gives an App Store or TestFlight app no API to read or tap another app. The route that does exist is the testing stack. An XCUITest runner, signed by a developer account and started through the phone's developer services, can read the accessibility tree of whatever app is in front and synthesise touches. phoneshell builds Appium's WebDriverAgent at the pinned tag v16.12.4, signs it with a free Apple developer account, installs it, and talks to it over HTTP bound to the phone's loopback. Its bring-up code names 16 checks, from xcode and developer-mode to wda-launch and wda-source, and bin/phoneshell doctor reports the ones that fail. Two phone toggles cannot be set from the Mac: Developer Mode, and Settings > Developer > Enable UI Automation. Without the second, the README warns, "everything installs, launches and silently does nothing".

The price shows up in operations. After a cold boot the phone sits in BFU, the state before the passcode is first entered, and developer services do not come up until a person types the passcode on the phone. WebDriverAgent is what would type the passcode, and WebDriverAgent cannot start. phoneshell's findings call this a security property rather than a bug. Their rule for unattended runs is that a reboot ends the session. Apple's own route is narrower and in-process: apps expose App Intents, which Siri can call with awareness of what is on screen, the subject of a WWDC26 session on 8 June 2026. That reaches only the actions an app's developer chose to expose.

The two gates, step by step

nanoMuse's Sentinel runs a fixed order: denied tools, the person's own rules, the always-allow and always-ask lists, taint, risk against the chosen mode, and last a set of warnings no list can wave through. For the screen rung it judges the model's sentence and the label at the landing point. On Android it also reads the accessibility text under the finger, and the stricter reading decides. A label with a committing word (confirm payment, transfer, place order, send, delete) makes the step sensitive, approved every time. "A typing step that submits in one go is sensitive, because Enter in a messenger is a send." A step whose target has no label carries a warning.

phoneshell's gate is 14 regular expressions in config.py, applied to the tapped element's label by phone_tap and phone_do. They cover order, pay, checkout, buy, purchase, send money, transfer, book, subscribe, delete, remove, log out and sign out. phone_type with submit=True writes an audit line and presses return, with no gate. The README says actions that look irreversible, naming "send", are refused the first time. The pattern list does not contain send. We found the same gap with different data on 5 October.

What it means if you want an agent to use someone's iPhone

  • There are three routes, and each costs something different. App Intents covers only what each app exposes. The XCUITest route covers every app with labelled controls, but needs a computer tethered to the phone, developer settings and a person after reboots. Android lets the agent live on the phone.
  • An agent that "runs on every device" has an asymmetric surface on iOS. nanoMuse's design of peers that use each other as tools fits this: a desktop peer could hold iPhone hands through the testing stack. On our reading of the report that is not built.
  • Gate the step that commits, whatever its form. On a phone that includes return in a text field and unlabelled icons, not only buttons whose label says pay. nanoMuse's list covers both. phoneshell's covers neither and should.
  • Report the number, and report what the number can see. phoneshell's 92.3% comes from the device rather than the model. Twelve of its tasks pass before the agent chooses anything, and without them the run scores 90.6%. nanoMuse's honest "no success rate" is better than a number nobody checked, and it leaves its hands unranked.

What would prove this wrong

Our claim is that iPhone hands for a third-party agent come only from outside the phone. By 31 March 2027 we expect nanoMuse's iOS app still to have no screen rung, and any iPhone hands it gains to run from a desktop peer through XCUITest or a similar testing route. If a TestFlight or App Store build of nanoMuse, or of any third-party agent, taps inside another app with no computer attached and no developer settings, the constraint as we state it is wrong. On our side, by 31 December 2026 phoneshell's gate will cover send and typing with submit. Rerun on the 388 audited taps from our earlier note, the widened list should flag at least one step the current one passes.
