GraceBench's top model passes 63.9% of tasks while completing 62.6% of the required path

GraceBench (arXiv:2610.19900, submitted 14 October 2026) reports that 51.24% of successful mobile-agent runs "still contain a deviation or loop." Its own Table 2 shows what the deviation is measured against. Under goal-only instructions GPT-5.6 Sol succeeds on 63.9% of 169 tasks while its mean completion of the required path is 62.6%. Spell the path out and the success rate stays at 63.9% while completion rises to 73.9%. Part of what the benchmark calls instability is distance from a reference route.

The paper is by Ridong Zhang, Shuxian Liang, Chaoqun Wan, Zhen Huang and Xian-Sheng Hua of the Institute of AI for Engineering, Tongji University. It runs eleven screenshot-only models on physical Android devices across 10 apps, records every episode with scrcpy, and maps each step onto a UI Transition Graph built beforehand by an exploring agent. Each task runs twice: with a detailed instruction that "follows one manually verified feasible path" and with a concise goal. That gives 3,718 trajectories, capped at 15 actions each.

Success is read from the run summary, completion from the graph, and the two disagree in Table 2

Appendix B defines the two outcome metrics differently. SR is "the proportion of runs marked successful in the frozen per-trajectory evaluation summaries." CR is completed required edges over required edges, averaged across all tasks, failures included. A failed run still earns partial CR, so mean CR should sit above SR. In 21 of the 22 model rows in Table 2 it does.

Rates in Table 2 are printed as decimals; we show SR, CR and DR as percentages. Eleven models, 169 tasks, 3,718 trajectories, 15-action budget, temperature 0.

GPT-5.6 Sol under the concise instruction: SR 63.9%, which is 108 of 169 tasks, and mean CR 62.6%. If every one of its failed runs had completed zero required edges, its successful runs would average a CR of 0.980 (our arithmetic: 0.6261 divided by 0.6391). Failed runs do not score zero, so the true figure is lower: some successes reached the target without walking the required edges. Appendix C.1 says as much: "a task can reach its target without completing every reference transition."

Across all eleven models, 426 model-task pairs succeed under both instructions. Inside those pairs, switching from detailed to concise lowers CR by 0.0591 (95% task-cluster interval -0.0879 to -0.0325) and raises DR by 0.0467 (0.0236 to 0.0713). Same model, same task, same successful outcome; only the route hint changed. For GPT-5.6 Sol the aggregate CR change is -0.1127 with an SR change of exactly zero.

A deviation label means the step left the mapped subgraph

Section 3.3 labels a step a deviation "if it leaves the task subgraph or enters" a state outside the application graph. The subgraphs include alternative routes and were reviewed by hand. The authors are also explicit that the graphs "do not imply exhaustive coverage" of the apps: 83 unresolved target observations were dropped from the transition counts, and a task's core is 5.91 states on average, 41.09 with one-hop neighbours. A working route outside that neighbourhood is labelled the same way as a wrong tab. So the 51.24% (663 of 1,294 successes) is an upper bound on wasted motion in successful runs. Deviation in successful runs rises from 36.78% under detailed instructions to 46.11% under concise ones, the direction expected if some of it is route choice.

Loops do not depend on which route is canonical: 20.66% of detailed successes and 27.48% of concise successes contain one, and 126 successful runs have zero deviation and still loop.

The headline about recovery is qualified in Appendix C.4

The abstract says GPT-5.6 Sol "achieves the highest success rate (SR) but the lowest recovery ability." Table 2 confirms both halves: SR 63.91% in both conditions, RA 0.4454 detailed and 0.4525 concise, last of eleven in both. RA is one over the steps taken to return to the path, zero if the agent never returns. Appendix C.4 tests whether it tracks outcomes. A return within three decisions is associated with SR 25.91% against 12.75% without one, on 1,035 matched trajectories. Yet of 1,301 trajectories that recovered from every deviation, 408 succeeded: 31.36%. And once a return is controlled for, a 0.1 gain in the speed score moves SR by 0.0047, with an interval of -0.0039 to 0.0133 that includes zero. The authors conclude the analysis does not support "an optimal latency function."

Step labels were generated by "expert agents" and audited by blinded humans on 313 trajectories, 3,487 of 34,767 transitions (10.0%). Cohen's kappa was 0.7980 for state match, 0.7952 for deviation, 0.7663 for recovery and 0.8656 for loop. The audit pool covered 3,042 trajectories. The other 676, every run of UI-TARS-1.5-7B and GUI-Owl-1.5-8B-Instruct, arrived with no video or screenshots and were excluded, so 18.2% of the dataset and both GUI-specific models have labels nobody could check against pixels.

A process score inherits its reference path

GraceBench's concise-instruction numbers are the ones closest to real use, and they are the ones where route distance and real detours are mixed. If you adopt DR, report it under the detailed instruction, and treat the concise-minus-detailed difference (+0.0522 over 1,859 pairs) as route divergence until someone audits the deviating successes. Loop burden travels better.

phoneshell has no reference path at all. A run record in phoneshell/bench/runner.py keeps passed, turns, seconds and cost_usd, and the leaderboard reports avg_turns. We cannot say what share of our passing runs wandered. We do have a loop definition that needs no graph: StuckDetector in phoneshell/agent/verify.py flags the same screen signature three times in a row, or an A, B, A, B, A, B alternation. That is the route-independent half of GraceBench's measure. Our judgement: it is the half worth logging per run first. The bench does not record it today.

What would prove this wrong

We claim that a material share of the deviation labels on successful GraceBench runs marks valid routes absent from the reference subgraph, not wasted actions. Our prediction: if the per-trajectory labels are public by 30 June 2027 and someone hand-reviews the successful concise-instruction runs that carry a deviation label, at least 20% of them will turn out to reach the goal by a legitimate route the subgraph did not contain. A share under 10% would show the 51.24% is nearly all real detour and this note is wrong.
