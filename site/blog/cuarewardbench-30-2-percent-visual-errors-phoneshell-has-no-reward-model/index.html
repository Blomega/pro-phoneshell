CUARewardBench's best reward model still misreads the screen on 30.2% of its own failures; phoneshell's stats module has no reward model to misread anything

CUARewardBench, submitted 21 October 2025, scores how well a vision-language model can judge whether a computer-use agent's trajectory succeeded. Its best method reaches 89.8% precision on outcome judgments, and when the authors read 53 of that model's own wrong calls by hand, 35.8% were reasoning errors and 30.2% were visual understanding errors: the judge itself cannot reliably see the screen it is grading. phoneshell's bench/stats.py answers a related question, whether one model beat another, without ever appointing a judge: it runs McNemar's exact test over which device-state checks each model actually passed.

Haojia Lin and eleven co-authors from the Youtu-Agent team published "CUARewardBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Reward Models on Computer-using Agent" (arXiv:2510.18596, submitted 21 October 2025) to fill a gap the authors describe as "largely underexplored": almost every computer-use agent paper that reports a reward signal either hand-waves the judge's own reliability or assumes a vision-language model can grade a trajectory as well as it can generate one. CUARewardBench tests that assumption directly. It covers seven agent architectures across ten software categories, with agent success rates across those architectures ranging from 25.9% to 50.8%, and evaluates both outcome reward models (ORM, which score a whole trajectory) and process reward models (PRM, which score individual steps) across seven vision-language models and three prompt templates.

What CUARewardBench's best judge gets wrong, and how often

The benchmark's own Table 2 splits its ground truth into 272 trajectory-level annotations (139 successful, 133 failed) and 346 step-level annotations (182 good actions, 164 bad). Against that ground truth, the paper's proposed method, Unanimous Prompt Ensemble (UPE), is the best performer: 89.8% precision and 93.3% negative predictive value for outcome judgments, falling to 81.7% precision and 85.1% NPV for the harder, step-level process judgments. The paper is explicit that action-level evaluation is harder than trajectory-level evaluation, and that general-purpose vision-language models beat models specifically trained as computer-use agents at this judging task, without giving a numeric margin for that comparison.

Section 4's failure analysis is the sharpest number in the paper. The authors hand-reviewed 53 failure cases produced by GLM-4.5V-106B, the strongest individual outcome reward model tested, and categorized every one. Reasoning errors accounted for 19 of the 53 cases (35.8%), and visual understanding errors accounted for 16 (30.2%). Together those two categories cover two thirds of every mistake the best outcome judge in the paper made. The remaining categories were action understanding errors (9 cases, 17.0%), knowledge deficiency (8 cases, 15.1%), and a single case of what the authors call an inherent reward-model limitation (1 case, 1.9%). A model built specifically to grade whether a computer-use agent succeeded is, by its own authors' accounting, wrong about what it saw on screen in nearly a third of its failures.

What phoneshell's stats module asks instead of appointing a judge

phoneshell's bench/stats.py exists because, in the module's own words, "the first version of this project computed [these statistics] in three places and published a leaderboard that did none of it. Six models sat on six percentages, three of them identical, and the page presented that as an ordering." Nothing in that file asks a vision-language model to look at a trajectory and render a verdict. Every outcome it compares is already a pass/fail produced by bench/verify.py's device-state checks, which read the accessibility tree, a switch's value, the foreground bundle ID, or OCR text directly off the phone, never a model's opinion of a screenshot.

Where CUARewardBench's best judge trades off precision against the two failure modes in the table above, phoneshell's stats.py trades off something else entirely: statistical power. Its wilson() function puts a 95% confidence interval on a raw pass rate rather than reporting the point estimate alone, because, as the module's docstring notes, "20/24 is 83.3%, but the 95% Wilson interval on it runs from 64% to 93%." Its mcnemar_exact() function compares two models over the same paired tasks using only the disagreements between them, and the module computes that it takes six one-directional disagreements, independent of how many tasks exist, before two models can be called different at p<0.05 at all. Neither function has an error rate of its own in the sense CUARewardBench's judge does: a Wilson interval and an exact p-value are arithmetic on the pass/fail counts, not a second model's perception of the first model's behavior that can itself be reasoning-wrong or visually wrong.

What it means for anyone comparing two agents on the same tasks

  • CUARewardBench's result is not an argument against reward models; it is a measured cost of using one. 89.8% precision at best, with a third of the remaining errors coming from the judge misreading the screen, is a real number a team should weigh against the alternative of not needing a judge at all.
  • phoneshell's alternative only works because its checks already read device state directly. A paired statistical test over pass/fail is only as trustworthy as the checks producing those pass/fail values, which is a different failure surface than a reward model's visual or reasoning error, not a strictly smaller one.
  • The comparison sharpens a real tradeoff: a reward model can grade free-form, open-ended trajectories a deterministic checker cannot describe in advance, which is exactly why CUARewardBench exists. phoneshell's paired-test approach is only available because every one of its 80 tasks ships with a checker that can decide pass/fail without seeing a trajectory at all.

What would prove this wrong

We claim CUARewardBench's Table 7 attributes 35.8% of GLM-4.5V-106B's 53 analyzed outcome-judging failures to reasoning errors and 30.2% to visual understanding errors; that is checkable directly against the paper's HTML version and would be false if a later revision changes that table. We also claim phoneshell's model-comparison statistics in bench/stats.py never invoke a vision-language model as a judge, which is checkable by reading the file in full: it imports only math. This piece will need revisiting if phoneshell ever adds a reward-model-style judge to its own pipeline, which would make this contrast obsolete, by 30 April 2027.
