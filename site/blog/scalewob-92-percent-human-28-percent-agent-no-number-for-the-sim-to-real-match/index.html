ScaleWoB's agents average 27.92% against a 92.08% human baseline; its only real-world check reports no number at all

ScaleWoB (arXiv:2605.25160, Tsinghua University's AIR, UESTC and Xiaomi's MiLM Plus) hands a coding agent app metadata and has it write 63 working mobile apps from scratch, then builds 120 tasks on top of them. Five evaluated agents average 27.92% success across those tasks, falling to 17.82% on the long-horizon subset, against a 92.08% human baseline. The paper's one check against the real world, 20 hand-built tasks on 17 real apps, supports its generalization claim with a sentence, not a statistic: no percentage, correlation or agreement figure for that comparison appears anywhere in the paper's text. phoneshell's own leaderboard carries a different sentence on its front page: "Every task runs on a physical iPhone. Nothing is simulated."

"ScaleWoB: Guiding GUI Agents with Coding Agents via Large-Scale Environmental Synthesis" (arXiv:2605.25160v2, 18 June 2026) describes a two-stage synthesis pipeline. Stage one has a coding agent build "a minimal working environment" from an app's metadata; stage two "injects task requirements" and "synthesizes executable validators for each task." The resulting apps are, in the paper's own words, "backend-free webpages," 63 of them, covering recognizable names like Gmail, Reddit, Spotify and Telegram, none of them the real thing. Task sourcing started from "260 valid requests from 86 participants across 77 industries," normalized and feasibility-screened down to 120. The authors are candid that the process has limits: these environments "cannot guarantee 100% usability," because the LLM that builds them "may contain flawed interaction logic or UI design issues," and "all environments and tasks are manually verified" before release specifically because of that risk.

A 120-task synthetic benchmark, and a 20-task spot check

ScaleWoB evaluates five agents on its 120 synthetic tasks, screenshot-only, since the paper states plainly that the environment "provides only screenshot observations and no structured UI signals," which means no accessibility tree was tested at all, only vision. Local open-weight models could not even be scored on the version of the task that reads back a value: "in preliminary trials, local fine-tuned models failed to produce schema-valid JSON outputs under prompting," so they were evaluated only on tasks without return values.

For comparison, the same five-model style of agent scores far higher on an existing benchmark built from real apps: the paper reports AndroidWorld scores in the range of "64.2%-71.6%" for agents in this class. ScaleWoB's own tasks are harder by construction, which the paper frames as a design goal rather than a flaw. What matters for the generalization question is a different comparison entirely: not synthetic versus AndroidWorld, but synthetic versus the paper's own 20 real-world tasks.

What "generalizes well" rests on

Section 4.2 states the real-world comparison directly: "we designed 20 similar tasks from 17 apps in a real-world environment and evaluated these agents. The results from the real-world environment were manually assessed to ensure accuracy." The conclusion drawn from that comparison is that "the performance trends observed on sample tasks from real apps are largely consistent with those of ScaleWoB, meaning the relative performance rankings among models remain the same," and the introduction summarizes this as showing that "agent assessments based on our synthetic environments generalize well." Read closely, the entire claim rests on ranking agreement across five agents and twenty tasks: which model beat which other model, not by how much, and not against any stated margin of error. No percentage, correlation coefficient, or agreement statistic for the 20-task comparison appears anywhere in the verbatim text of the paper. Reordering five models correctly on a 20-item sample is a far lower bar than matching an absolute success rate, and the paper does not claim to have cleared the higher bar, only the lower one.

phoneshell's own front page states its position on this question in one sentence, written for a different purpose but directly on point here: "Every task runs on a physical iPhone. Nothing is simulated, and no human decides whether a run passed." There is no staged environment anywhere in phoneshell to validate against reality, because there is no second environment: every task that has ever appeared on phoneshell's leaderboard ran, and was graded, on the same physical iPhone 17 Pro Max the house thesis describes. The tradeoff ScaleWoB accepts, generating 63 apps cheaply and checking a 17-app, 20-task sample against reality, buys scale phoneshell does not have. phoneshell's tradeoff buys a leaderboard with nothing to validate, because nothing on it was ever generated.

What it means for reading a sim-to-real claim

  • "Rankings stay the same" and "absolute scores stay the same" are different claims, and only the first is what ScaleWoB actually measured. A benchmark that wants to say more than "model A still beats model B" needs either a larger real-world comparison set or a stated statistic, not twenty tasks and a sentence.
  • ScaleWoB's scale is the real advantage here and should not be understated: 120 tasks across 63 apps, sourced from 86 real users across 77 industries, is a genuine attempt to cover breadth no physical-device benchmark can match cheaply. The cost of that breadth is a validation step this piece can only describe as thin, by the paper's own numbers, or rather by the paper's own absence of numbers.
  • phoneshell's alternative is not free either: a benchmark that only ever runs on one physical device cannot scale to 63 apps overnight, and its own thesis openly trades breadth for the certainty that every score on its leaderboard is a score on the real thing, not a score on a prediction about the real thing.

What would prove this wrong

We claim ScaleWoB's abstract and Section 4.2 report a five-agent average of 27.92% overall and 17.82% long-horizon success against a 92.08% human baseline, and that its real-world validation section contains no percentage, correlation or agreement statistic for the 20-task comparison, both checkable directly against the cited arXiv v2 HTML. We also claim phoneshell's bench/site.py states every leaderboard task runs on a physical device with nothing simulated, checkable in that file. This should be revisited if a later ScaleWoB revision adds a quantitative real-world agreement figure, or if phoneshell ever adds a simulated task source of its own, by 30 June 2027.
