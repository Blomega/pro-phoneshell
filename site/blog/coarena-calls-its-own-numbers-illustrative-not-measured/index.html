CoArena proposes judging computer-use agents live; its own paper calls every number in it illustrative, not measured

CoArena (arXiv:2609.14239, submitted 13 September 2026) proposes judging computer-use and multi-agent systems by running two of them on the same live, user-submitted task at once, inside identical sandboxed desktops, and letting a human who does not know which system is which decide the winner. The votes feed a Bradley-Terry leaderboard that updates online. The paper formalizes a five-system, 211-vote worked example all the way through to ratings, confidence intervals, and rank bands. Then it says, in its own words, that none of it is a measurement of anything deployed: “every number is derived from stated inputs or labeled illustrative; none is a measurement of a deployed system.”

A formal spec for a live arena, carried through a worked example that is not a sample

The paper, "CoArena: Evaluating Computer-Use and Multi-Agent Systems in Real Time," is by Nitish Kovuru and Prateek Jannu, posted to arXiv on 13 September 2026 as a single version, 29 pages, 9 figures, 4 tables, 4 algorithm listings. Its core proposal: real users submit tasks, two systems (each a single model or a multi-agent pipeline behind the same tool interface) execute the same task concurrently in identical sandboxed desktops, a human judges the two outcomes blind, and a public leaderboard is refit from those judgments. The paper's stated contribution is a formal account of what makes an evaluation genuinely "real-time," broken into five properties: continuous task arrival, live concurrent execution, online rating updates, freshness with contamination resistance, and bounded feedback latency from a failed run to a reusable training environment. Ratings run on the Bradley-Terry pairwise model, with a streaming update the authors describe as a stochastic-gradient step on the same likelihood, one that recovers the familiar Elo update when a single vote arrives.

To show the machinery working, the paper carries a five-system example with 211 votes from a vote matrix through ratings, confidence intervals from a parametric bootstrap, rank bands, and inter-judge agreement statistics. That is exactly the pipeline a real deployment would need. The paper's own sentence attached to that example states plainly that it is not one: "every number is derived from stated inputs or labeled illustrative; none is a measurement of a deployed system."

The same two authors are not purely academic about this. Coasty Research Lab, trading as CoArena, is a Y Combinator S26 company running coarena.ai, a live site where people watch two computer-use models attempt the same real task and vote blind on which did better, the product version of the paper's design. A 29 August 2026 item on runtimewire.com, by Ryan Merket, reported that "CoArena's site currently says new tasks and judging are paused," about two weeks before the methodology paper itself was posted. The article gives no battle counts, vote totals, or per-model success rates for the live product, so none of those figures can be checked against the paper's framework, and they are not used anywhere below.

What the five properties are meant to fix, and what they do not yet show

Each of the five real-time properties targets a specific, named failure of the fixed benchmarks CoArena is positioned against: continuous task arrival keeps the question set from going stale; live concurrent execution keeps both systems facing the same task conditions at the same moment; online rating updates mean the leaderboard never waits for a batch; freshness with contamination resistance is meant to stop a model from having trained on the exact evaluation tasks; and bounded feedback latency ties a failed run back into a reusable training environment quickly enough to matter. The streaming Bradley-Terry update is the mechanism that makes "online" more than a word: a single new vote nudges every rating by a stochastic-gradient step on the pairwise likelihood, rather than requiring a full batch refit.

What a measured arena needed before it was informative

The worked example is small next to what a different, already-deployed arena needed before its own leaderboard was treated as informative. Computer Agent Arena, an unrelated ICLR 2026 project also evaluating computer-use agents by live blind comparison, collected 2,201 high-quality votes across 12 agents before publishing. CoArena's worked example is a tenth of that vote count, over fewer than half as many systems, and is explicitly not drawn from real votes at all.

What it means for reading an evaluation-methodology paper

A paper that formally specifies an evaluation design is not evidence that the design works at the scale it is meant for. Computer Agent Arena, a different team's live arena already covering computer-use agents, needed 2,201 votes across 12 agents before its own authors treated the resulting leaderboard as informative, more than ten times the 211-vote worked example CoArena uses to demonstrate its math. CoArena's five properties are a reasonable list of what a contamination-resistant, always-fresh leaderboard should guarantee, and the streaming Bradley-Terry update is a real mechanism, not a hand-wave. But a reader citing this paper for "CoArena found X" is citing an illustrative worked example, by the authors' own description, not a result. The live product by the same authors existing, and having reportedly paused new tasks and judging as of late August 2026, is a separate fact worth knowing before assuming the design has already been running at scale somewhere else.

What would prove this wrong

We claim that CoArena's abstract (arXiv:2609.14239v1) states its reported numbers, including the five-system, 211-vote worked example, are "stated inputs or labeled illustrative" rather than measurements of a deployed system, and that runtimewire.com reported coarena.ai's new tasks and judging as paused on 29 August 2026, both checkable directly against the cited sources. This should be revisited if coarena.ai or a follow-up paper from the same authors publishes a deployed vote count at or above Computer Agent Arena's 2,201, with the five real-time properties measured against a live log rather than defined in the abstract, by 30 September 2027.
